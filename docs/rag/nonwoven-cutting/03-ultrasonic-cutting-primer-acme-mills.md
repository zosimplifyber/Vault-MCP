## Operating frequency of ultrasonic cutting

Acme Mills: "The blade pulses 20,000 – 40,000 vibrations per second (20 – 40 kHz)."

## Resonance tuning in ultrasonic cutting

The cutter is tuned to the specific frequency at which a given material responds best.
This is called resonance, and it is what makes the vibration efficient rather than merely
present.

The transducer carries a feedback-control system that reports the optimal frequency back
from the cutter automatically, holding resonance alignment during the run so results stay
consistent as the material and blade heat up.

**Practical consequence:** resonance tuning is a per-material setup step. A process window
established on one web does not transfer to a web of different basis weight, density or
fibre.

## Components of an ultrasonic cutting system

| Ultrasonic cutting system component | Function of that component in ultrasonic cutting |
|---|---|
| Generator in an ultrasonic cutting system | Supplies electronic AC current that is converted into telescopic motion at ultrasonic frequency. It powers the transducer, which is what removes the need for heavy blade pressure. |
| Transducer in an ultrasonic cutting system | Converts the electronic energy into vertical mechanical oscillation matching the frequency of the high-frequency AC current. Carries the feedback control that maintains resonance. |
| Cutter or blade in an ultrasonic cutting system | Moves in response to the transducer oscillation. The oscillation heats and softens the material, and the blade parts it. |

## Materials Acme Mills lists for ultrasonic cutting

Fabric, natural and synthetic fibres, plastic, rubber, piping, frozen meat, candy,
jewellery, and printed circuit boards.

**Read this list with care.** Acme Mills lists "natural and synthetic fibers" without
qualifying a thermoplastic requirement, which reads as though non-melting natural fibres
are fully supported. But the mechanism Acme Mills describes on the same page is thermal:
the oscillations "heat and soften materials for cutting," and the sealed edge comes from
"vibration-induced heating."

Treat the unqualified natural-fibre listing as an omission rather than as evidence that
the sealing behaviour extends to non-thermoplastic fibre. Rinco and SinapTec both qualify
the same claim.

## SinapTec — the explicit thermoplastic statement

SinapTec states the technology is for "thermoplastic textiles or nonwovens containing
thermoplastic fibers," where the ultrasonic energy causes "controlled melting at the
cutting zone."

SinapTec's mechanism description: a generator powers a transducer that converts electrical
energy into high-frequency mechanical vibration, transmitted to a cutting tool called a
sonotrode. On contact with the textile, "ultrasonic vibrations generate highly localized
heat."

On the cut-and-seal behaviour: "the ultrasonic blade does more than just cut — it
simultaneously seals the edges," giving "clean cuts with sealed edges, without using
adhesives, threads, or heat-sealing tapes." The claimed benefits are significantly reduced
fraying, prevention of fibre unravelling, and improved edge mechanical strength.

On heat damage: the heat-affected zone "remains extremely limited," which preserves the
bulk material properties compared with a conventional heated blade that can cause surface
damage.

SinapTec publishes no specific frequency, power, pressure, speed or sonotrode geometry
figures, and instead refers to "optimized frequency, power, and pressure parameters
tailored to your application." SinapTec's stated application span runs from high-speed
medical mask lines to geotextile panel cutting.

## Edge quality and application fit for ultrasonic cutting

Acme Mills claims "clean lines and minimize waste," with vibration-induced heating creating
sealed seams that eliminate fraying.

Acme Mills specifically positions ultrasonic cutting for "thick fabrics or fabrics with an
uneven weave," where laser cutting produces a poor finish. This is a useful distinction:
the two sealing methods are not interchangeable across web structures.

## Acme Mills method overview — the six methods for nonwoven fabric

| Cutting method in the Acme Mills nonwoven overview | Material fit Acme Mills gives for that method | Mechanism of that method |
|---|---|---|
| Rotary cutting of nonwoven fabric | Thick material or multiple layers | Rotating circular knife; "maintaining an extra-long and sharp grind on the circular blade" ensures precision |
| Razor slitting of nonwoven fabric | Thin nonwovens on spools, high-speed operation | Material tensioned on a rewinder and cut by fixed industrial blades; blade coatings reduce heat |
| Plotter cutting of nonwoven fabric | Complex designs and intricate shapes | CNC with an oscillating knife on a vacuum table; "high precision and accuracy" |
| Die cutting of nonwoven fabric | Specific shapes produced by punch-out | Material placed between a die and a cutting press; serrated knife option for straight cuts |
| Ultrasonic cutting of nonwoven fabric | Complex shapes and patterns | High-frequency vibration applied to a cutting knife; speed "far exceeds that of traditional oscillating knives" |
| Laser cutting of nonwoven fabric | Medical and hygiene products such as surgical masks and gowns | High-powered laser through the material; clean cuts on delicate detailed material |
