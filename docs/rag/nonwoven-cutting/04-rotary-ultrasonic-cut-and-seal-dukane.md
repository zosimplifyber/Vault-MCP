## How rotary ultrasonic cut-and-seal differs from blade ultrasonic cutting

This is the inverse of blade-based ultrasonic cutting, and the distinction matters when
reading parameters across the two.

| Aspect of ultrasonic cutting | Blade-based ultrasonic cutting | Rotary ultrasonic cut-and-seal |
|---|---|---|
| What defines the cut profile in this ultrasonic method | The blade, that is the sonotrode itself, carries the cutting edge | The **patterned steel anvil** carries the profile; the rotary horn is plain |
| Tooling change to alter the cut in this ultrasonic method | Change the blade or sonotrode | Change the anvil |
| Web handling in this ultrasonic method | Sheet or roll, intermittent or plotted contour | Continuous web through a nip |
| Typical speed of this ultrasonic method | 25 m/min continuous textile cutting, per Rinco | Up to 2700 fpm, approximately 823 m/min, per Dukane |

**The patterned anvil defines the cut and seal profile, not the horn.** Dukane's
description: "ultrasonic cutting is accomplished by using high-frequency vibration against
an anvil with a specially designed continuous edge that causes materials to melt away."

## Line speed for rotary ultrasonic cut-and-seal

Dukane: "High speed intermittent or continuous processing - up to 2700fpm."

2700 feet per minute is approximately 823 metres per minute — roughly 33 times the 25 m/min
Rinco quotes for continuous blade-based textile cutting. The two figures describe
different processes and should not be compared as if they were alternatives for the same
job.

## Material requirement — explicitly melt-based

The mechanism is stated as melt-and-separate: the anvil edge "causes materials to melt
away." This is an unambiguous thermoplastic dependency. A non-melting cellulosic web has
nothing to melt away, so the rotary cut-and-seal mechanism does not transfer.

Dukane also claims "effective bonding of dissimilar materials" and the ability to "bond
seams on garments where a continuous seam without holes is required."

## The basis-weight economic argument

Dukane's commercial case is not speed, it is material cost:

"Ultrasonic technology offers high-precision process control, which enables effective
cutting of very lightweight nonwoven materials, which allows the utilization of less
expensive, lighter weight nonwoven materials."

The argument is that precise process control lets you run a lighter, cheaper web than a
less controlled process would tolerate, and the saving comes from the basis weight rather
than from the line rate.

## Other characteristics Dukane claims

- Low power consumption
- Operates in contact with other surfaces to maximise pressure
- Simple, low maintenance system design

## What Dukane does not publish

are hygiene and personal care products, particularly garment seaming and edge formation on
nonwovens.
